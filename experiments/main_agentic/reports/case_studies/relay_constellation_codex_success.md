# Codex on Relay Constellation

This case study diagnoses why the `codex` harness surpassed the recorded solver baselines on `relay_constellation` in the `main_agentic` report. The evidence is anchored to tracked artifacts: aggregate outcomes in `experiments/main_agentic/reports/relay_constellation.md`, solver baseline rows in `experiments/main_solver/README.md`, the exposed relay workspace contract in `experiments/_fragments/prompts/relay_constellation/README.default.md`, the relay scoring function in `experiments/_shared/score_normalization.py`, solver documentation under `solvers/relay_constellation/`, and Codex trace exports under `experiments/main_agentic/reports/traces/data/events/relay_constellation__codex__test__case_000*.js`.

## Outcome

Codex's relay result is a real win, but it is not a uniform per-case domination. In the aggregate report, Codex is valid on all five cases with mean `service_fraction = 0.9352` and mean normalized score `64.91`. The two recorded solver baselines are also valid on all five cases: `relay_constellation_mclp_teg_contact_plan` averages about `60.04` normalized points, and `relay_constellation_umcf_srr_contact_plan` averages about `59.95`. Codex wins the normalized mean mainly because it fully serves case 0003 with two added relays. The UMCF/SRR baseline comes very close on that case (`service=0.9911`, `worst_service=0.9667`) but does not reach full service; Codex reaches `service=1.0` and `worst_service=1.0`, so the normalized scorer allows the satellite/latency bonus and the score jumps to `89.23`.

## Contract Discipline

Codex's relay traces show strong authority discipline. In every case it reads the README early, writes a minimal valid `solution.json` before optimizing, uses the Brahe skill or installed Brahe package for orbital propagation, and repeatedly checks candidates against the local verifier. The earliest traces are explicit: case 0001 says it will put a valid deliverable in place before optimization, then writes an empty `added_satellites` / `actions` file and verifies it; case 0002 probes `./verifier --help` and a placeholder solution; case 0003 says it will create a baseline deliverable before generated candidates; case 0004 checks the verifier on an empty or missing solution to lock down JSON shape; case 0005 reads the README, probes verifier behavior, and only then builds the solver.

That behavior matters because relay is an easy benchmark to solve in the wrong interface. The space agent does not submit routes, service assignments, or latency claims; it submits only physical link activations. The verifier constructs the active graph, checks per-sample feasibility, and allocates demand routes. Codex internalizes this. Its generated scripts produce `added_satellites` plus `ground_link` and `inter_satellite_link` intervals, then treat verifier metrics as the final answer. There is no sign of a private verifier overriding the packaged verifier. Codex does use private geometry and routing estimates, but it treats them as search heuristics.

## What Codex Built

Codex does not reproduce either first-party solver architecture. The solvers use broader candidate libraries and method-family pipelines: MCLP plus route-aware time-expanded contact scheduling, and UMCF/SRR over path-restricted LP relaxations. Codex instead behaves like a case-specific network engineer. It reads the small demand set, identifies outage windows or backbone partitions, synthesizes a small number of bridge relays from geometric reference points, and emits exact link intervals for the demanded windows.

The generated relay designs are targeted rather than generic. In case 0001, Codex names candidate roles such as `bridge` and `endpoint`, builds one bridge between `backbone_005` and `backbone_006` plus one endpoint-facing relay near `ground_004`, and finalizes a 62-action two-relay plan. In case 0002, it finds that the first 800 km, 85 degree relay clears major service gaps, then adds a second 1400 km, 85 degree relay to resolve the remaining five unique missed samples and the `demand_001` / `demand_003` overlap trade. In case 0003, it uses two augmenting relays named `augment_35_210_120` and `augment_55_090_120`, then refines the pair for lower latency while preserving full service. In case 0004, it settles on one 1499 km, 55 degree relay tuned to a Madrid outage and a backbone partition. In case 0005, it builds a five-relay set from targeted bridge points such as `A`, `C`, `E0`, `H1`, and `G1`, where the fifth relay only marginally improves service but is retained because the case remains below the full-service gate and service is still the primary objective.

This is why Codex can outperform a reproduced solver on a few public cases without being a stronger general relay solver. The task instances have small demanded-window sets and clear bottleneck structure. A targeted, verifier-in-the-loop search can find a compact bridge design that a conservative library-based solver may miss under its promoted candidate grid and scheduling policy.

## Scoring Mechanics

The normalized relay scorer in `experiments/_shared/score_normalization.py` makes an important distinction. If `service_fraction < 1.0`, the score is `70 * (0.75 * service_fraction + 0.25 * worst_demand_service_fraction)`. Satellite count and latency do not help until the solution reaches full service. Only full-service solutions can receive the remaining 30 points from added-satellite scarcity and latency.

This means case 0003 dominates the story. Codex reaches full service with two added relays and low latency, so it earns the 70 service points plus a large bonus. UMCF/SRR is almost there, but `0.9911` service is still below the gate, so it remains at about `68.95`. That is not an arbitrary loophole: full service is a meaningful relay-network threshold. A single unserved demand sample can matter operationally, and the benchmark intentionally makes perfect service qualitatively better than almost-perfect service. But it does mean that a small absolute improvement near 1.0 can produce a large normalized jump.

For the other four cases, Codex is below full service, so its satellite efficiency and latency are not rewarded by the normalized formula. Case 0001 and case 0004 are clear losses despite using fewer relays, because the benchmark rightly values service before architecture efficiency. Case 0002 is a cleaner service win: Codex has higher service and higher worst-demand service than both baselines. Case 0005 is roughly a wash; Codex's final service is slightly below MCLP and very close to UMCF/SRR.

## Why This Is Not Just Luck

Codex repeatedly catches objective and allocation nuances that are easy to miss. In case 0001 it recognizes that overlapping service between `demand_001` and `demand_002` should favor the shorter window because the reported service fraction is per-demand normalized, not just total served minutes. In case 0004 it makes the same kind of correction: "the primary score is average demand fraction, not total served minutes," so overlap minutes are more valuable on the shorter `demand_003` window. In case 0002 it notices the same overlap structure and biases the conflict so `demand_001` is full while `demand_003` takes the smaller normalized loss. Those are benchmark-aligned scheduling decisions, not accidental geometry hits.

Codex also does the right thing when its local model and verifier disagree. It starts from empty or backbone-only baselines, observes verifier outputs, adjusts link actions and relay states, and re-runs the verifier. In case 0003, one candidate is invalid at seq 454, then Codex repairs and reaches `valid=true`, `service=1.0`, `worst=1.0` at seq 495, and later improves latency while preserving full service. In case 0005, it compares a four-relay verified candidate around `0.8976 / 0.6000`, tests a fifth bridge, sees the estimated gain to `0.8988 / 0.6083`, then writes and verifies the five-relay final solution.

## Why It Is Still Bounded

The success is case-adaptive and trace-time, not a reusable solver replacement. Codex writes scripts inside each workspace, searches hand-selected geometric bridge patterns, and uses the verifier as the feedback oracle. That is powerful on small public cases with few demanded windows, but it depends on enough time to inspect, script, verify, and manually target the bottlenecks. The first-party solvers are less nimble on these exact cases but more systematic: they have documented candidate generation, scheduling, debug artifacts, reproducibility envelopes, and standalone solver contracts.

There is also a ceiling visible in Codex's losses. On case 0001 and case 0004, Codex chooses compact relay sets that preserve architecture efficiency but leave too much service on the table. Since the scorer gives no satellite-count bonus below full service, these are not hidden wins. They are normal service-quality losses. The benchmark's ordering is doing what it should: service first, worst-demand robustness second, then augmentation efficiency and latency.

## Takeaway

Codex succeeds on `relay_constellation` because it combines strong contract discipline with targeted bottleneck hunting. It reads the README, uses Brahe rather than hand-waving orbital geometry, keeps a valid `solution.json`, and lets the local verifier arbitrate. Its main normalized advantage comes from case 0003, where a compact two-relay bridge plan reaches full service and crosses a scoring threshold that the reproduced solvers narrowly miss. The broader lesson is not that Codex has a better general relay optimizer; it is that on small, structured relay instances, a verifier-guided evaluated space agent can identify a few high-leverage bridge relays and sometimes beat a conservative, reproducible baseline on the exact benchmark objective.
